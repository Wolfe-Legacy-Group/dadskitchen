The most important thing you do at the dinner table isn't cook. It isn't serve. It isn't even sit down. It's ask a question that can't be answered with "yes," "no," or "fine" — and then close your mouth.

That's it. That's the whole skill. And it's harder than it sounds.

## The right question opens a door

There's a difference between "How was school?" and "What's something that happened today that surprised you?" The first one gets you "fine." The second one makes a kid think. It invites them to tell a story instead of giving a report.

Open-ended questions work because they don't have a right answer. There's no pass or fail. Your kid isn't being quizzed — they're being asked to share something, and the question itself tells them you're genuinely curious.

Here are a few that work well at our table:

- "What's something you changed your mind about recently?"
- "If you could make one rule at school, what would it be?"
- "What's something you're better at now than you were a month ago?"
- "Who did you help today — or who helped you?"

You'll find the ones that work for your kids. The key is that the question invites a real answer, not a one-word escape hatch.

## Plant the seed while you cook

Here's a move that works well: bring up the question during the cooking, not at the table. While you're chopping or stirring, casually say something like, "I've been thinking about this — I want to ask you something at dinner. Think about what the hardest part of your week was."

Now your kid is thinking about it while they set the table. By the time you sit down, the answer isn't cold — it's been warming up. They've had time to think instead of being put on the spot, and the question feels like something you've both been looking forward to, not an interrogation.

## Then stop talking

This is the hard part. You asked the question. Your kid started talking. And now you need to be quiet.

Not "quiet while planning what you're going to say next." Actually quiet. Listening. Making eye contact. Letting them take the conversation wherever it goes — even if it wanders, even if it circles back, even if they end up somewhere you didn't expect.

Because here's what's happening when you stay quiet: your kid is learning that their thoughts matter. That the table is a place where they're not just allowed to talk — they're the reason everyone is listening. That makes them feel valued. It makes them feel like a grown-up at the table, not a kid who's been given permission to speak.

That feeling — *what I say matters here* — is what brings them back to the table next time. Not the food. The feeling.

## What not to do

Don't correct. Don't redirect. Don't turn their answer into a lesson. If your kid says something you disagree with, let it sit. You can talk about it later, or not at all. The table is for connection, not correction.

And don't fill the silence. If your kid pauses, wait. They might be thinking. They might be figuring out how to say something they've never said before. That silence isn't empty — it's working.

## It gets easier

The first few times, it might feel awkward. Your kid might give short answers. They might look at you like you're being weird. That's fine. Keep asking. Keep listening. Keep being quiet.

Over time, the table becomes a place where talking is normal — where your kid expects to be asked something real and expects to be heard when they answer. That doesn't happen in one dinner. It happens over dozens of dinners where you showed up, asked a good question, and let them talk.
