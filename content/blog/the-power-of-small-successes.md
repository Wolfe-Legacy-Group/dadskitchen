There's a moment every parent knows. Your kid does something. Cracks an egg without getting shell in the bowl. Flips a pancake without it folding. Carries a full glass of water to the table without spilling. And they look up at you with that face. *Dad, did you see that?*

But here's the version that hits different: you saw it before they asked. You were already watching. And you said something first.

"You got it."

Two words. No fanfare. But your kid just learned something that will stay with them longer than any trophy or report card: *My dad pays attention to me. He notices when I do things right. He cares enough to watch.*

## Why "before they ask" matters

When a kid says "Dad, watch this!" and you look up and say "nice job," that's good. That's parenting. But when you notice on your own, when you name the thing they did before they had to point it out, you're telling them something deeper. You're telling them they don't have to perform to be seen. They don't have to compete for your attention. You're already there.

That's a different kind of confidence. It's not the confidence that comes from being praised. It's the confidence that comes from being known.

## Small wins build big confidence

A kid who successfully measures a cup of flour isn't going to remember that specific moment in ten years. But a kid who has a hundred small moments where they did something right and someone noticed? That kid walks into new situations differently.

They try out for the team. They raise their hand in class. They introduce themselves to someone new. Not because they're fearless, but because they've built a long track record of trying something, succeeding at something small, and having that success recognized by someone who matters to them.

That track record started somewhere. It can start in your kitchen.

## The kitchen is built for this

What makes the kitchen work for small successes is that everything is a task with a visible result. You crack an egg. It either has shell in it or it doesn't. You stir the batter. It either has lumps or it's smooth. You set the table. The forks are either on the right side or they're not.

These aren't abstract achievements. They're concrete, immediate, and obvious. A kid can see their own success without anyone explaining it to them. And when you see it too, when you name it before they have to, you turn a small kitchen task into proof that effort leads to results and that someone is paying attention.

## Start watching before they ask

Next time you're in the kitchen together, try this: don't wait for them to show you something. Watch what they're doing. When they get something right, even something tiny, name the specific thing they did. Not a generic "good job." The specific action you saw.

"Look at you double-checking that measurement. That's how you make sure it turns out right."

"I noticed you rinsed off a couple bowls while we had some downtime. That's going to save us a lot of time on cleanup later. Thanks for thinking of that."

"You leveled that off perfectly. That's exactly how you do it."

The difference matters. Kids are smart enough to tell when your acknowledgment has depth and when it's empty. A vague "nice job" might feel good for a second, but a specific observation tells them you were actually paying attention. That you saw the choice they made, not just the result. That's what sticks.

Then keep going. Don't make it a moment. Just let it land. They'll carry it further than you think.
